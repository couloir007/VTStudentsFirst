Vermont's school redistricting fight is happening right now, in real time, in Montpelier. If you live in a tuitioning community — or in any Vermont town with a school — the decisions being made this session will shape what education looks like in your area for decades.

Here's where things stand.

## What Act 73 requires

Vermont's 2025 education reform law, Act 73, created a hard deadline: the legislature must vote on a new map of school districts during the 2026 legislative session.

If lawmakers adopt a new map, it would trigger a cascade of changes:
- Vermont's 119 school districts would consolidate into larger units, with new boundaries taking effect in the 2028–2029 school year
- A new foundation funding formula — allocating per-pupil dollars from the state rather than through local property taxes — would take effect alongside the new districts
- Control over school budgets would shift substantially from local school boards to Montpelier

If lawmakers do not adopt a map, the foundation formula does not take effect, and most of Act 73's structural reforms stall. Some provisions — class size minimums, independent school eligibility rules — take effect regardless.

## What the redistricting task force actually recommended

Act 73 created a School District Redistricting Task Force to do the legwork: draw up to three possible maps for the legislature to consider, holding public hearings along the way. The task force, which included legislators, former superintendents, and education policy experts, met through the summer and fall of 2025.

In December, the task force delivered its report — and rejected its mandate.

Rather than drawing new district maps, the task force's majority endorsed a 10-year plan for voluntary mergers, incentivized through a new state school construction aid program and structured around five cooperative education service regions. The minority, led by Sen. Scott Beck (R-Caledonia) and Gov. Scott's appointee Dave Wolk, had proposed a map of 14 districts aligned with Vermont's career and technical education regions. But that map was rejected.

Governor Scott called the task force's failure to produce a district map "a political strategy to preserve the old system." Task force members said they simply didn't have enough time to do the work responsibly.

Three members of the task force had direct ties to the Northeast Kingdom: Sen. Scott Beck of Caledonia, Rep. Beth Quimby of Lyndon, and Dr. Jennifer Botzojorns, a former Kingdom East superintendent.

## The maps now on the table

With the task force having failed to deliver, the legislature's education committees have taken up the mapping work themselves.

**Conlon's map (House).** Rep. Peter Conlon, the Democratic chair of the House Education Committee, introduced a draft map in January 2026 that would consolidate Vermont's 119 districts and 52 supervisory unions into approximately 27 supervisory districts, each with 2,000 to 4,000 students. Conlon has acknowledged the map is "faulty" and "fraught with political decisions" — it's explicitly a starting point, not a finished proposal. Notably, his policy language would effectively eliminate school choice in communities that currently have it, directing districts to assign a designated school for each student rather than allowing family choice.

**Bongartz's map (Senate).** Sen. Seth Bongartz, the Democratic chair of the Senate Education Committee, has proposed a framework targeting no more than 11 supervisory unions and 12 supervisory districts statewide, with 56 school districts — roughly halving the current structure. His proposal preserves school choice. The State Board of Education would be empowered to force mergers in a third year if voluntary consolidation stalled.

As of early March 2026, the two committees had not reconciled their approaches. Conlon's committee is struggling to find consensus even among its own members, and Conlon has suggested the committee may need to ask for dispensation on the legislature's crossover deadline.

## What this means for the Northeast Kingdom

For the NEK, the redistricting debate is inseparable from the school choice debate.

The region's towns are among the roughly 90 in Vermont that currently participate in tuitioning — that is, they pay tuition for students to attend the school of their family's choice, rather than operating their own public high school. Under Conlon's proposal, that choice system would be replaced by a district-assignment model. Families would no longer choose; the district would designate.

That's not a technical adjustment. For communities in Caledonia, Essex, and Orleans counties, it's a fundamental change in how school has worked for 150 years.

Bongartz's proposal takes the opposite position — that school choice in tuitioning communities should be preserved. But his proposal has its own complications, and neither chamber has moved toward a floor vote.

The Beck/Wolk minority map, which would have created a single NEK-area district preserving choice, was rejected by the task force's majority. Elements of it may resurface in legislative negotiations.

## What happens if the legislature doesn't vote on a map

If the legislature fails to adopt new district boundaries by the end of the 2026 session, Act 73's foundation formula does not take effect. Property taxes continue under the current system. School consolidation does not happen on the Act 73 timeline.

This is not a hypothetical outcome. As of early March 2026, there is genuine uncertainty about whether the legislature will be able to find the votes for any map — or whether the political resistance to mandatory consolidation will prove too great.

Governor Scott has insisted that no aspect of Act 73's funding reform can work without larger districts. "We don't see a scenario where the foundation formula we established last year would work well at all with 119 districts," his policy director said in January. But some legislators argue that funding reform and governance reform can be decoupled — that Vermont could fix the property tax problem without forcing district mergers.

That argument has not yet produced a bill.

## What we're watching

The legislature's 18-week session is more than halfway through. Here's what to watch:

**Crossover deadline.** Bills need to pass their originating chamber before crossover in order to move to the other chamber in the same session. Conlon has signaled he may need an extension on the education committee's work.

**Joint deliberations.** The House and Senate education committees are working to reconcile their approaches. Whether they can do so — and on what terms for school choice — is the central question of the remainder of the session.

**The vote.** If a map makes it to the floor of both chambers and passes, new districts are scheduled to begin operation in 2028–2029. If it fails or stalls, most of Act 73's transformation is delayed indefinitely.
