# What Happens to Family Choice Under Vermont's New School Districts?

**slug:** vermont-school-choice-new-districts  
**schema_description:** Vermont's redistricting proposals would fundamentally reshape — or eliminate — school choice in communities that currently have it. Here's what the competing proposals mean for families in tuitioning towns.  
**schema_topic:** Vermont school choice 2025, Vermont education reform family choice, Vermont tuitioning redistricting  
**schema_citation:** "Conlon's proposal effectively eliminates school choice in the approximately 90 communities that currently have it." — Vermont Public, March 2026

---

Vermont has roughly 90 communities where families choose where their children go to school. These are the "tuitioning towns" — places where the school district doesn't operate its own school for some or all grades, and where state law requires the district to pay tuition for students to attend the school of their family's choice.

For these communities, the redistricting debate isn't just about governance structures. It's about whether family choice continues to exist at all.

## How choice works now — and why it matters

In a tuitioning town today, a family whose child is entering high school can choose from any state-approved independent school or public school in a neighboring district. The district pays the tuition, up to the statewide average announced tuition. If the family's preferred school charges more than that cap, they pay the difference.

This is not a new program. It's how school has worked in Vermont's rural communities for 150 years. And in practice, it means that families in towns like Sheffield, Wheelock, Concord, Newark, or Granby have real choices — not theoretical ones. They can choose St. Johnsbury Academy or Lyndon Institute or a neighboring public school, and that choice is publicly funded.

The redistricting proposals before the legislature would change that.

## What the Conlon map would do to choice

Rep. Peter Conlon's draft map, introduced in January 2026, would create approximately 27 larger districts. Within those districts, the current tuitioning model would be replaced.

Under Conlon's accompanying policy language, each district would designate a specific public or private school for each grade level if there is no "reasonably accessible public school" operated by the district. That designated school would be under contract with the district and subject to a set of public-school-equivalent requirements.

The key shift: families would no longer choose. The district would designate, and students would attend the designated school.

Conlon has framed this as a way to preserve the historic academies — St. Johnsbury Academy and Lyndon Institute are explicitly contemplated under the designated school model — while bringing greater accountability and ending what he views as an inefficient and inequitable choice system.

But the result, for families in tuitioning communities, is a fundamental change in their relationship to their children's school. Under the current system, if a family has a reason to prefer one school over another — academic program, proximity, a sibling already enrolled, a special circumstance — they can act on that preference. Under the designated school model, that flexibility is gone.

## What the Bongartz proposal would do

Senate Education Chair Sen. Seth Bongartz has proposed a competing redistricting framework that preserves school choice. His proposal would restructure governance — creating larger supervisory unions and reducing the total number of districts — without eliminating the tuitioning system in communities that currently have it.

Bongartz has said eliminating choice is a "nonstarter" in his chamber. The independent school community has a stronger foothold in the Senate, where geographic representation gives the NEK and Bennington County — both heavily tuitioning — more weight than in the House.

The catch is that Bongartz's proposal hasn't moved either. As of early March 2026, the two chambers are far apart, and the clock is running.

## The "designated school" model — what it means in practice

The Conlon designated-school framework deserves a closer look, because it's being framed as a preservation of the academies when its practical effects are more complicated.

Under the model, a district would contract with a school — potentially St. Johnsbury Academy or Lyndon Institute — to serve as the designated school for students in that district without a local public option. That school would need to meet significant requirements: open enrollment, no selective admissions for publicly funded students, no application or academic fees, progress reporting to the district, notice before suspensions or expulsions.

For the large academies, some of these requirements are manageable. But the designated-school contract structure changes the relationship between the school and the families it serves. The family's choice is now the district's contract, not the family's decision. The school is now accountable primarily to the contracting district, not to the parents who chose to send their children there.

That is a different institution than the one that has served the NEK for 150 years.

## What would happen to families who want a different school

Under the designated-school model, families who want to send their children to a school other than the district's designated school would presumably do so on their own dime. The publicly funded pathway would go to the designated school, not to the family's preferred alternative.

For most families in tuitioning communities, the designated school would likely be one of the large academies they'd have chosen anyway. But "likely" is not "always." And for families with specific reasons to prefer a different school — religious affiliation, academic specialization, proximity, personal history — the loss of choice is real.

The grandfather clause in Act 73 protects students already enrolled in independent schools whose eligibility changed. There is no equivalent protection for the elimination of the choice model itself.

## The redistricting question for tuitioning towns specifically

Here's the practical question that hasn't been answered: what happens to the 90-plus communities that currently tuition, once redistricting creates larger districts?

Under Conlon's map, many of those communities would be absorbed into a larger district that does operate public schools. At that point, by Act 73's logic, the new district would no longer be required to pay tuition — because the district now has a public school. The family's child would be expected to attend the district's public school, not a private academy.

Whether that's what Conlon intends is somewhat unclear. His designated-school language tries to preserve a pathway for independent schools. But the details matter enormously, and the details aren't settled.

Under Bongartz's approach, tuitioning communities would retain their choice system within the new supervisory structure. The status quo for families would largely continue.

## Why this is the fight that matters most

For families in the NEK, everything else in the Act 73 debate is secondary to this question: when the dust settles, will my family still be able to choose where my children go to school?

The funding formula matters. Class size minimums matter. District governance matters. But none of it matters as much, on a daily lived basis, as whether the family in Wheelock can still choose St. Johnsbury Academy, or whether the family in Sheffield can still send their kids to Lyndon Institute.

Those choices are not preferences. They are the reason some families are in Vermont at all. And if the legislature eliminates them — not through some explicit policy statement, but through the accumulated effect of district redesign, designation mandates, and the slow narrowing of approved schools — the families who would have left will leave, and the towns that were already losing people will lose them faster.

This is what we mean when we say the stakes are demographic. Vermont's rural communities cannot absorb another generation of outmigration. The legislature needs to understand that clearly before it votes.
