# St. Johnsbury Academy and Lyndon Institute: Why They Matter to the Whole NEK

When people in other parts of Vermont hear "private school," they picture something specific: a campus in the hills, a lacrosse team, an endowment. A school for families who could afford something else.

St. Johnsbury Academy and Lyndon Institute are private schools in the technical sense — they are not operated by a school district. But anyone who lives in the Northeast Kingdom, or who has tried to understand how education actually works here, knows they are something else entirely.

They are the public schools of the NEK. They just don't happen to be owned by the state.

## What these schools actually are

St. Johnsbury Academy was founded in 1842. It sits at the center of St. Johnsbury, Vermont's largest town in the Northeast Kingdom, and serves students from across the region. Its total enrollment typically runs around 900 students. The majority attend on publicly funded town tuition — they come from the dozens of surrounding towns that have no high school of their own.

Lyndon Institute, founded in 1867 in Lyndonville, operates similarly. It serves students from Lyndon, Burke, Sheffield, Wheelock, and other towns across Caledonia County. Like St. Johnsbury Academy, it was built to be the regional high school for an area where individual towns were simply too small to operate their own.

These are not schools that compete with public schools for students. They are the schools that exist because public schools couldn't.

That distinction is not just historical. It is the entire legal and moral foundation of Vermont's Town Tuition Program. The state has paid tuition to these schools for generations because the alternative — building separate public high schools in each of the small towns that send students here — would cost far more and serve far fewer students.

## The economic engine argument

When parents and community members defend these schools, they sometimes lead with curriculum, athletics, or extracurriculars. Those arguments are real. But the more important argument is economic, and it rarely gets made clearly.

Schools like St. Johnsbury Academy and Lyndon Institute are not just educational institutions. They are anchor institutions — the kind of employer, civic presence, and community attractor that determines whether a rural region can sustain itself.

St. Johnsbury Academy employs hundreds of people. Its campus draws families to the area. Employers who are considering relocating to the NEK ask about schools before they ask about property taxes. Young couples with children ask about schools before they sign a lease. A region with a strong, stable secondary school is a region that families consider moving to. A region without one is a region they leave.

Vermont is already losing the demographic fight. Enrollment in Vermont's schools has been declining for years, and the NEK has felt that loss more sharply than most. Fewer children means fewer workers, fewer taxpayers, fewer small businesses, fewer young families buying houses and joining civic organizations and coaching Little League.

Restricting or destabilizing the schools that anchor these communities doesn't save money. It accelerates the decline.

## What families have told us

In the survey that formed the foundation of this coalition, parents from across the NEK described what St. Johnsbury Academy and Lyndon Institute mean to them — not in policy terms, but in personal ones.

Families told us directly: if their children could no longer attend these schools under the Town Tuition Program, they would leave Vermont. Not reluctantly. Not as a negotiating position. As a straightforward account of what their family needs to stay here.

This is the consequence that doesn't appear in the legislative analyses. The funding formula spreadsheets don't have a line item for families who pack up and move to New Hampshire because Vermont made their school unavailable. But those families are real, and so is the ripple effect when they go: the house that goes vacant, the job that goes unfilled, the tax base that shrinks a little more.

## The "elite private school" misconception

Critics of Vermont's tuitioning program sometimes frame these schools as benefiting wealthy families at taxpayer expense. That framing has always been inaccurate in the NEK, and it's worth addressing directly.

The families who send their children to St. Johnsbury Academy and Lyndon Institute on town tuition are not, in the main, wealthy families who could afford to pay out of pocket. They are the families of farmers, tradespeople, small business owners, and service workers who happen to live in a tuitioning town. The tuition program is what makes these schools accessible to them at all.

The ProPublica framing — that wealthy Vermonters game the voucher system to send their kids to Phillips Exeter — applies to a small subset of out-of-state prep school placements. It has almost no relevance to St. Johnsbury Academy or Lyndon Institute, where the student body is drawn almost entirely from the region, and where the school's mission has always been to serve those students.

When the legislature talks about "limiting the flow of public dollars to private schools," it is using language that fits urban choice programs. It does not fit what is happening in St. Johnsbury or Lyndonville.

## What class size minimums threaten

Act 73's new class size minimums — which take effect for the 2026–27 school year — apply to independent schools receiving public tuition, including the major NEK academies.

For St. Johnsbury Academy and Lyndon Institute, the minimums are manageable. Their enrollment levels give them the flexibility to comply without gutting their course offerings.

But the minimums create a harder problem for the smaller schools that fill gaps in areas where even the main academies are too far away. If those smaller schools lose eligibility or close, the students they serve don't automatically have somewhere else to go. They end up with fewer choices, longer bus rides, or no viable option at all.

## What H.813 would add

H.813, a House bill introduced in early 2026, would go beyond Act 73's restrictions to impose a sweeping set of public-school-equivalent requirements on independent schools receiving public funding. The bill would extend Vermont's Education Quality Standards to all approved independent schools, along with open enrollment mandates, public records and open meeting obligations, student discipline requirements, special education staffing mandates, and financial transparency requirements.

The bill hasn't moved yet. But it signals where a portion of the legislature wants to go: toward treating independent schools, as a condition of receiving public tuition, as functionally equivalent to public schools — with all the administrative, regulatory, and compliance costs that implies.

For schools like St. Johnsbury Academy and Lyndon Institute, some of those requirements are reasonable and already partially met. Others would fundamentally alter the character of institutions that have served their communities for 150 years.

## Why "just build a public school" isn't an answer

The question is sometimes asked: why not simply build public high schools in the NEK and end the dependence on independent schools?

The answer is arithmetic.

St. Johnsbury Academy serves students from dozens of surrounding towns. Distributing that enrollment across multiple separate public high schools in those towns would produce schools with enrollment in the dozens — too small to staff adequately, too small to offer the academic breadth or extracurricular opportunities that families value, and too expensive per student to justify.

The NEK has always known this. That's why these schools exist. The region didn't build independent academies as a workaround — it built them as the most sensible solution to the geographic and demographic reality of rural Vermont.

That reality hasn't changed. What has changed is that Montpelier is now writing laws designed primarily for school districts with thousands of students, and applying them to a region where the entire model is built around something different.

## What we're asking for

We're not asking the legislature to abandon education reform. Vermont's school funding system has genuine problems, and rising property taxes are a real burden on real families.

What we're asking is that the reforms being crafted in Montpelier account for what Vermont actually looks like in the Northeast Kingdom — and that any solution preserve the schools that make it possible for families to stay here.

St. Johnsbury Academy and Lyndon Institute aren't obstacles to reform. They are proof that it's possible to build excellent, community-rooted, financially sustainable education in one of the most rural parts of New England. The legislature should be asking how to replicate that model — not how to regulate it out of existence.
