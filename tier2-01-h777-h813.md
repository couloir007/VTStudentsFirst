# H.777 and H.813: The Next Threat to Vermont Independent Schools

Act 73 was not the end of the Vermont legislature's work on independent schools. Two bills introduced in the 2026 session — H.777 and H.813 — would add new layers of restriction and regulation on top of the changes already enacted last year.

Neither bill has moved to a vote as of early March 2026. But both represent the direction a portion of the legislature wants to go, and both deserve close attention from families in tuitioning communities.

## H.777: An athletics scholarship program with strings attached

H.777 would create a new scholarship program for student athletes attending approved independent schools on publicly funded tuition.

On its face, that sounds like a benefit. But the bill's structure is notable for what it requires schools to do to participate:

Schools offering the athletics scholarships would need to demonstrate academic credit transferability to Vermont public schools, allow participation in statewide longitudinal tracking, consent to independent evaluation of outcomes, and submit annual public reporting of data to the Agency of Commerce and Community Development. Athletic programs would face new requirements around certified coaching staff, athlete-to-coach ratio caps, and safety and risk-management protocols. Scholarship retention would require evidence of athletic progress, judged by an independent annual performance review panel made up of non-academy reviewers.

The bill is framed as athlete support. But read in context — alongside H.813 and the broader push to impose public-school requirements on independent schools — it functions as another vector for state oversight of schools that are not, and have not been, state institutions.

The bill takes effect July 1, 2026 if passed.

## H.813: The accountability bill that would reshape independent schools

H.813 is the more consequential bill. It would impose a sweeping set of requirements on all approved independent schools receiving publicly funded tuition — requirements that currently apply only to public schools.

Under H.813, any independent school receiving public tuition would be required to:

**Meet Vermont's Education Quality Standards (EQS).** These are the standards that govern curriculum, staffing, and academic performance in public schools. Independent schools have historically operated under their own quality frameworks.

**Comply with open enrollment requirements.** Schools could not refuse enrollment to publicly funded students. Combined with the prohibition on selective admissions practices, this would change the admissions model at schools that currently exercise selectivity for privately paying students.

**Prohibit selective admissions and fees.** Schools could not conduct mandatory interviews or require academic entrance exams for publicly funded students, and could not charge application or academic fees.

**Meet public records and open meeting obligations.** Portions of school governance and finances would become subject to the same transparency requirements as public school boards.

**Meet student discipline protections.** Schools would be required to notify sending districts before suspending or expelling publicly funded students.

**Comply with special education staffing requirements.** Schools would need to employ qualified special education staff to serve students with IEPs.

**Meet labor protections.** The bill would extend certain labor standards applicable to public school employees to independent school staff.

**Provide expanded financial transparency.** Schools would need to attest annually that public tuition funds are not used to subsidize private-pay students, and provide additional financial reporting to the state.

The intent, as proponents frame it, is accountability: if public dollars flow to a school, that school should be accountable to the public in the same ways public schools are.

The concern, as independent school advocates frame it, is that these requirements would fundamentally alter the character and operating model of schools that have served their communities well for over a century — adding administrative costs, reducing admissions flexibility, and applying a one-size-fits-all regulatory framework to institutions built on a very different model.

## How these bills relate to the redistricting debate

H.777 and H.813 don't exist in isolation. They're part of a broader 2026 legislative conversation about what role independent schools should play in Vermont's education system going forward.

Rep. Peter Conlon's redistricting map proposal, discussed in the House Education Committee since January, would effectively eliminate school choice in communities that currently have it by redesigning districts to ensure all students have a "reasonably accessible public school." Conlon has framed independent schools as a cost problem for the broader system — one that redistricting can help contain.

H.813 is the parallel track: if redistricting doesn't eliminate independent school participation, H.813 would reshape the terms on which they participate. The two bills together represent a pincer movement — one working through governance structure, the other through statutory requirements.

Senate Education Chair Sen. Seth Bongartz has proposed a competing redistricting framework that preserves school choice. But as of early March 2026, the two chambers remain far apart, and the crossover deadline is approaching.

## What this means for NEK families

For families in the Northeast Kingdom, the relevant question is what these bills would mean for St. Johnsbury Academy, Lyndon Institute, and the other schools their children attend.

St. Johnsbury Academy and Lyndon Institute already meet many of the requirements H.813 would impose — they are large, well-resourced institutions with long records of accountability to the communities they serve. But the compliance costs of formal EQS requirements, public records obligations, and expanded state oversight are real, and they fall unevenly: they're manageable for large schools, harder for smaller ones.

The more fundamental concern is what these bills signal about the legislature's long-term direction. Each year, another layer of requirement. Each session, a new proposal to narrow the definition of which schools qualify, or to impose new conditions on those that do.

At some point, the question is no longer whether an independent school can comply with the latest set of regulations. It's whether the school — as a school, with its own identity, governance, and community culture — can survive the cumulative weight of them.
