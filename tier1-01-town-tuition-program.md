# What is Vermont's Town Tuition Program — and why is it at risk?

Vermont has had school choice longer than most states have had public high schools.

Since the mid-1800s, towns that are too small or too rural to operate their own schools have paid tuition for local children to attend another school — public or private — of their family's choosing. It's called the Town Tuition Program, and for generations it has been as Vermont as maple syrup and town meeting. Today, roughly 95 towns use it for high school students, and many more for younger grades.

For families in the Northeast Kingdom, it's not an educational curiosity. It's how school works.

## How the program works

Under Vermont law, school districts that don't operate a school for certain grade levels are required to pay tuition for their resident students to attend another school. Families in those "choice towns" can select from approved public schools in neighboring districts or from a list of approved independent schools — what Vermont calls private schools that have been vetted by the state.

The tuition amount is capped at the statewide average announced tuition. For the four historic academies that anchor the Northeast Kingdom — St. Johnsbury Academy, Lyndon Institute, Thetford Academy, and Danville's St. Johnsbury Academy branches — tuition rates are set close to that cap, making the program work seamlessly for most NEK families.

There are no income limits. Any family in a tuitioning town can use the program, regardless of what they earn.

## What makes it different from vouchers elsewhere

When national media covers school choice, they typically mean new programs created to shift students out of existing public schools. Vermont's program is the opposite. It exists precisely because public schools don't exist in those communities. The state isn't subsidizing a private alternative to a public option — it's funding the only option there is.

Vermont's tuitioning system has been called the longest-running and largest school choice system in the country, born of necessity in small towns where it simply wasn't feasible to build and staff a traditional public high school.

That history matters. The program didn't emerge from a political agenda. It emerged from geography.

## What Act 73 changed

In July 2025, Governor Phil Scott signed Act 73 — Vermont's sweeping education reform law. The legislation was primarily aimed at consolidating the state's 119 school districts and reforming how schools are funded. But tucked inside were significant new restrictions on which independent schools can participate in the Town Tuition Program.

Under the new rules, an independent school must clear two main hurdles to remain eligible for public tuition:

The school must be located within a district that does not operate a public school for at least some grade levels. And at least 25% of its students must have been publicly funded during the 2023–24 school year.

The effect was dramatic. The number of private schools eligible to receive public tuition dollars dropped from 46 to 18 statewide. All 12 Catholic schools in Vermont lost eligibility. Dozens of schools that had served tuitioning students for years were cut off.

Jon Olsen of the Vermont Independent Schools Association called it the biggest reduction in access to independent schools in the history of Vermont's education system.

There is a grandfather clause: any student already enrolled, or accepted for enrollment before July 1, 2025, can finish their education at their current school under the old rules. But younger siblings, and any new student from a tuitioning town, face a narrower set of options going forward.

## What's still at stake in 2026

Act 73 created a School District Redistricting Task Force to draw new district maps for the legislature to approve in 2026. If the legislature adopts a new map, it would trigger a sweeping funding formula change and district consolidations scheduled to take effect in 2028–2029.

A proposed map from House Education Committee Chair Rep. Peter Conlon would effectively eliminate school choice in the approximately 90 communities that currently have it. Senate Education Committee Chair Sen. Seth Bongartz has proposed a competing approach that preserves choice — but the two sides remain far apart.

As of early 2026, the House Education Committee has not even decided whether to proceed with redistricting, let alone agreed on what a new map should look like, while property tax bills have risen roughly 40% over the past five years.

Meanwhile, in the House, H.813 would impose sweeping new accountability requirements on independent schools receiving public funding — including open-enrollment mandates, public records obligations, and education quality standards that currently apply only to public schools. The bill would extend Vermont's Education Quality Standards to all approved independent schools receiving public funding, along with open enrollment requirements, prohibitions on selective admissions, public records and open meeting obligations, student discipline protections, special education staffing requirements, labor protections, and expanded financial transparency requirements.

## Why NEK families are watching closely

The Northeast Kingdom has more at stake than most regions. Disagreements over independent schools often fall more along geographic than party lines, since the schools have the biggest presence in Bennington County and the Northeast Kingdom.

Schools like St. Johnsbury Academy and Lyndon Institute don't just serve tuitioning students — they anchor the regional economy, attract young families, and give the NEK a secondary education infrastructure it otherwise couldn't sustain. For communities in Burke, Lyndonville, Barton, and dozens of towns across Caledonia, Essex, and Orleans counties, these schools are not an alternative to public education. They are what public education looks like here.

That's why this coalition exists. The decisions being made in Montpelier right now will determine whether the families who have always called the NEK home can still build a life here.
