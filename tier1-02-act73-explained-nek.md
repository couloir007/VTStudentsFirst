# Act 73 Explained: What It Means for NEK Families

Last summer, Vermont passed the most significant education reform law in a generation. Act 73 made national headlines for its ambitions: consolidate school districts, overhaul school funding, and set Vermont on a path toward a leaner, more equitable education system.

The statewide coverage focused on property taxes, funding formulas, and governance structures. What it largely missed was what this law means for specific places — towns like Burke, Lyndonville, and Barton where independent schools aren't an ideological preference. They're the only option.

This is the explanation Montpelier isn't giving you.

## What Act 73 actually does

Act 73, signed by Governor Phil Scott on July 1, 2025, is a multi-year framework that touches nearly every aspect of how Vermont's public schools are governed and funded. Its major components:

**District consolidation.** The law created a School District Redistricting Task Force to propose new maps merging Vermont's 119 school districts into larger units. If the legislature approves a map in 2026, new districts would begin operating in the 2028–2029 school year.

**A new funding formula.** If — and only if — the legislature adopts new district maps, Vermont would shift from its current property-tax-based system to a per-pupil "foundation formula" that allocates a base amount per student, with additional weights for poverty, special education, and English language learners.

**Class size minimums.** Beginning in July 2026, all Vermont schools — public and independent alike — must meet new minimum class sizes. These range from an average of 10 students per classroom in first grade to 18 students in high school grades. Schools that fail to meet the minimums for three consecutive years can be closed, though a waiver process exists.

**Statewide graduation requirements.** The law establishes uniform proficiency-based graduation requirements for high school students across Vermont.

**New restrictions on independent schools.** This is the piece that landed hardest in the NEK, and the piece that statewide coverage consistently underexplained.

## What changed for independent schools on July 1, 2025

Before Act 73, Vermont had 46 independent schools approved to receive public tuition dollars through the Town Tuition Program. After July 1, that number fell to 18.

To remain eligible, a school now must meet two conditions:

First, it must be located within a school district that does not operate a public school for at least some grade levels. Second, at least 25% of its enrolled students must have been publicly funded during the 2023–24 school year.

The effect of the second rule was to disqualify schools that primarily serve privately paying families, even if they also enroll tuitioning students. It also disqualified every Catholic school in Vermont — all 12 of them.

The four major NEK academies — St. Johnsbury Academy, Lyndon Institute, Thetford Academy, and others — survived because they meet both tests: they sit within non-operating districts, and a significant share of their students are publicly funded. St. Johnsbury Academy is explicitly listed among the eligible schools by the Vermont Agency of Education.

But the restrictions matter for NEK families in ways that aren't always obvious. A family in a tuitioning town in Orleans County, looking at options beyond the main academies, now has fewer choices. A younger sibling of a student at a school that lost eligibility cannot follow their older brother or sister there on public funds.

## What the grandfather clause actually covers

Act 73 includes a grandfather clause, and it's important to understand exactly what it does and doesn't protect.

Any student who received publicly funded tuition at an approved independent school in the 2024–25 school year can continue receiving publicly funded tuition at that same school until they graduate — even if the school no longer meets the eligibility criteria. The same protection applies to students who were accepted for enrollment before July 1, 2025.

What it does not protect: any new student from a tuitioning town who wants to attend a now-ineligible school starting with the 2025–26 school year or after. For those families, the options are narrower, and the costs — if they want to attend their preferred school anyway — come entirely out of pocket.

## The redistricting question that hasn't been answered

Act 73 created a hard deadline: the legislature must vote on new district maps during the 2026 session, or major provisions of the law — including the new funding formula — will not take effect.

The problem is that as of early March 2026, the legislature has not come close to agreement.

The redistricting task force delivered a report in December that sidestepped its mandate to draw new maps, recommending voluntary mergers instead. Governor Scott called the failure to produce a map "a political strategy to preserve the old system."

House Education Chair Rep. Peter Conlon has proposed a map that would create about 27 districts — and would effectively eliminate school choice in the 90-plus communities that currently have it. Senate Education Chair Sen. Seth Bongartz has countered with a proposal that preserves school choice. The two committees have not reconciled their differences. Conlon himself has acknowledged his committee hasn't yet decided whether to proceed with mandated consolidation at all.

What this means for NEK families: the situation is not settled. The biggest decisions about whether your children's schools will still receive public tuition money — and whether those schools will be large enough to stay open — are being made right now, this session, in Montpelier.

## The class size problem hiding in plain sight

One provision of Act 73 that receives less attention than it deserves: the class size minimums apply to independent schools, too.

Beginning in the 2026–27 school year, any school receiving public tuition dollars must meet the same average class size floors as public schools: 10 per classroom in first grade, 12 in grades 2 through 5, 15 in grades 6 through 8, and 18 in grades 9 through 12.

For larger academies like St. Johnsbury and Lyndon Institute, this is manageable. For smaller independent schools that serve tuitioning students in more rural areas, the requirement is potentially existential. A school that can't meet the minimums for three consecutive years can be directed to close by the State Board of Education.

## What's still being fought over

Act 73 was never the end of this fight — it was the beginning of the next one. Here's what's still live:

**The redistricting vote.** The legislature must act this session. The outcome will determine whether Vermont's new funding formula ever takes effect, and whether school choice survives in its current form.

**H.813.** This House bill, recently introduced but not yet taken up, would extend Vermont's Education Quality Standards — and a sweeping set of accountability requirements — to all independent schools receiving public funding. It goes further than anything in Act 73.

**Two active lawsuits.** The Liberty Justice Center filed suit in March 2026 on behalf of two Vermont families, arguing that Act 73's eligibility restrictions violate the Vermont Constitution. A separate challenge from Mid Vermont Christian School, backed by Alliance Defending Freedom, alleges that the law discriminates against religious schools. Both cases are in Vermont Superior Court.

**The federal wildcard.** Two bills — H.770 and S.161 — are working through the legislature to decide whether Vermont will participate in a new federal education funding mechanism, and on what terms. The decision would further shape which schools can receive public dollars.

## What Burke and Lyndonville know that Montpelier doesn't

In the NEK, the conversation isn't abstract. It's about specific families who moved here, or who are deciding whether to stay here, in significant part because of the schools.

It's about the family in Burke whose kids would have attended Burke Mountain Academy if a neighboring school loses its eligibility. About the family in Barton weighing whether to stay in Vermont or move somewhere their children's school options aren't subject to annual revision in Montpelier. About towns that understand, from long experience, that when you take away the school, you take away the reason to stay.

The state's statewide reformers have a problem to solve: rising property taxes, declining enrollment, consolidation that is genuinely necessary in some parts of Vermont. We don't dispute that. But the solution being applied to Chittenden County is not the same solution that works in Caledonia County. And every family in the NEK who has watched their neighbor leave the state already knows it.
